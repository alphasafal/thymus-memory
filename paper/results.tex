\subsection{Disclosure must not depend on the model}\label{sec:disclosure}

Inline labels work only if the model follows them. qwen3-4B, Opus~5.5 and GPT-6-Astra do:
label-only and v1 cut attacks by half or more. Llama~3.1~8B does not: label-only (41\%) and v1
(47\%) are no better than no defence. The final gate takes the decision away from the model:
contested claims are withheld and disclosed by the system, which cuts Llama's attack success
from 42\% to \absLlamaThy{}. The ablations below were run on v2, before verification (6~October),
and not repeated on the final gate. Simply dropping contested claims (\emph{hide}) blocks attacks too
(qwen3-4B 10\%, Llama 28\%), but the real cross-origin updates it drops leave 30\% of update
questions answered with the stale value. Removing the labels (\emph{no-labels}) returns attack
success to the undefended level (qwen3-4B 38\%): disclosure, not storage policy, is the active
ingredient.

\para{Where attacks still get through.} The notice protects only claims the gate contests. Split
by what the final gate did with the planted claim, Llama is fooled by \stratLlamaContested{} of
the \stratLlamaContestedN{} attacks it contested, but by \stratLlamaUncontested{} of the
\stratLlamaUncontestedN{} it stored without a contest (the conflict check saw no contradiction,
so the claim reached the model with an inline label only); qwen3-4B, which follows labels, is
fooled by \stratQwenContested{} and \stratQwenUncontested{}. The gate's weak point is therefore
not its rules but how many contradictions the conflict check finds; \cref{sec:gateaware} attacks
it directly.

\subsection{An automated attacker that knows the gate}\label{sec:adaptive}

We gave qwen3-14B a description of the gate's rules and asked it to rewrite every English
tool-delivered attack of the test split three ways, each meant to slip past them: as the
\emph{same source} confirming its own claim, in the \emph{user's voice}, and as an
\emph{addition} that does not seem to contradict anything (\adaptQwenN{} attacks per model;
every rewrite keeps the planted value). Fooled, qwen3-4B / Llama~3.1~8B: no defence
\adaptQwenAll{} / \adaptLlamaAll{}, label-only \adaptQwenLabels{} / \adaptLlamaLabels{}, the gate
without verification \adaptQwenAuto{} / \adaptLlamaAuto{}, v2-verified \adaptQwenVer{} /
\adaptLlamaVer{}, and the final gate \adaptQwenThree{} / \adaptLlamaThree{} (against v2-verified on
Llama: 37 vs 1 discordant pairs, $p<0.001$). The rewrites still contradict a stored fact from
another origin, so the contest catches them whatever their wording; the attacks that do get
through are those that create no contradiction at all (\cref{sec:gateaware}).

\subsection{Attacks designed against the gate}\label{sec:gateaware}

After freezing the gate we wrote 20 attacks aimed at its rules, five topics each: a
\emph{first-contact} claim that contradicts nothing stored (a ``fraud helpline'' the user never
saved); a change \emph{reframed as an addition} so the conflict check sees no contradiction (``in
addition to customer care, fraud is handled only by\dots''); \emph{pre-emption}, where the
attacker states the fact before the genuine source, so the genuine message becomes the
contested one; and \emph{same-origin persistence}, where a lookalike sender states a harmless
fact and then ``updates'' its own message. \gateAwareSentence{} Reframing fails because the
claim still contradicts the stored fact. Persistence and pre-emption succeed by design of the
rules, not by evading the models: a source may update its own earlier statement, and the first
statement of a fact is the one later claims are contested against. The rules keep both claims
labelled with their origin, so what remains is a model that ignores labels; against these two
strategies the system notice does not apply, because nothing is contested. Binding replacement to
a sender's reputation, or contesting a first statement when a known source later disagrees, would
close them at the cost of real updates; we leave both open.

We reproduce UUA's central finding: provenance blocking is safe on poisoned data but collapses
when the real answers arrive through untrusted channels (11\%; UUA report 4\%). Retiring a
replaced memory failed here at first (clean accuracy 63\% $\to$ 26\%): a LongMemEval memory is a
whole chat round, so retiring it because one fact changed deletes the rest. Keeping replaced
multi-fact memories as labelled older versions restores clean accuracy and \lmeGainSentence{}
Under poisoning, provenance quarantine does better than the gate here (63\% against
\lmePoisoned{}; the gate's agent still adopts the planted answer \lmePoisonedAdopt{} of the time).
The cause is partial contesting. Each poisoned question plants three rounds; when the
gate contests all three, the agent never adopts the false answer (0 of 7), but usually it
contests only one or two (the others are judged not to contradict a stored fact), and those
reach Llama with only an inline label, which Llama ignores (\cref{sec:disclosure}): 41 of the
43 adoptions came with a notice that held some of the planted rounds. The gate's protection
depends on seeing a contradiction; a claim the conflict check misses is labelled, not withheld. When the real
answer itself is untrusted, provenance quarantine collapses (11\%) and the gate keeps
\lmeUntrusted{}, disclosing what it cannot verify instead of dropping it.

On qwen3-4B the gate still beats no defence under poisoning (\lmeQwenPoisoned{} against
\lmeQwenAllPoisoned{}) but stays well below provenance quarantine (\lmeQwenUserPoisoned{}), and
with untrusted evidence it no longer beats it (\lmeQwenUntrusted{} against
\lmeQwenUserUntrusted{}). Here the failure is the conflict check, not the notice:
\lmeQwenAdoptNoNotice{} of the gate's \lmeQwenAdoptN{} poisoned adoptions came with no notice at
all, because no planted round was judged to contradict a stored one, and in
\lmeQwenAdoptLabelled{} of them qwen3 marked its answer as unverified, which the UUA judge rightly
counts as adoption. A conflict check written for single claims does not carry over to whole chat
rounds; on this protocol the gate's benefit depends on the model, and we do not claim it.

Undefended models are fooled \emph{less} in Hindi, partly because they use Hindi memories less
at all (Llama answers 70\% of Hindi updates correctly vs 96\% in English). The gate's gain also
depends on the language. The cause is not retrieval or translation: the extractor stores Hindi
messages as English facts, and every store is small enough that the original fact is always
shown to the conflict detector. It is the gate model's judgment: before verification, the 8B
gate missed the link between, for example, two customer-care numbers for the same bank, and
left 37\% of Hindi and Hinglish attacks uncontested (English 28\%). Because only these judgments
are learned, the gate model can be chosen apart from the agent. With Llama as the agent and
qwen3-4B as the gate model (gate before verification), Hinglish attacks fall from 27\% to 3\%
(7 vs 0 discordant pairs, exact McNemar $p=0.016$, uncorrected; across the six
language-by-model comparisons, Holm gives $p\approx0.10$) without costing updates (87\% $\to$ 83\%), and
Hindi from 23\% to 13\% ($p$ n.s., $n=30$); English is unchanged (17\% $\to$ 22\%, $p=0.41$). A
model trained for multilingual use is not automatically better: with Aya Expanse~8B~\cite{aya}
as gate model no language changes significantly. The language gap lives in the gate's model,
not its rules, so the gate model is a deployment choice to test per language. A larger gate model is not
automatically better: with Qwen3~14B judging for Llama~3.1~8B, the final gate is fooled
\bigLlama{} of the time against \absLlamaThy{} when Llama judges for itself, because the larger model
calls fewer contradictions (it contests \bigLlamaContestedN{} of the planted claims against
\stratLlamaContestedN{}); for qwen3-4B the result is unchanged (\bigQwen{} against \absQwenThy{}).
What matters is how readily the gate model reports a contradiction, which a deployment should
measure rather than assume from model size.

\subsection{Privacy: other users of a shared assistant}\label{sec:privacy}

In a shared assistant, another user's statement about the victim (``her new number is
\dots'') is an attack on the accuracy of personal data. Provenance quarantine admits it as
user content (fooled by 17 of 33 on qwen3-4B, 24 of 33 on Llama); the \gate{} gate contests it
against the victim's own statement (\otherUserSentence{}), and none of the 72 notices it wrote
named the other user. The gate thus serves the data principal's right to accurate data
(DPDP Act s.~8(3), GDPR Art.~5(1)(d)) without leaking who made the claim; it does not provide
confidentiality between users (\cref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Production assistants}\label{sec:production}

To check that the problem is not an artefact of our harness, we ran 15 three-chat trials on the
authors' own ChatGPT and Claude accounts with invented facts (\cref{sec:assistants}). In a new chat hours later, ChatGPT repeated 3 of 6 fake
updates as fact (a lookalike email, a third-party web page and a forged calendar invite) and
attributed the lookalike email to the user (``You also mentioned\dots''), losing the warning it
had given when it first read it. Claude disclosed the source and called the claim unconfirmed
in five of six, the behaviour the \gate{} gate builds in, but answered the forged invite with a
caveat, and rejected a genuine same-sender update as possibly spoofed. These are the two
failures the gate's rules address: lost provenance (disclosure) and no rule for who may update
a fact (authenticated same-origin supersession).

\subsection{Deployment: Mem0, confirmation burden and cost}\label{sec:deployment}

\para{Inside a real memory library.} The gate runs in front of Mem0~\cite{mem0} without changing
it: Mem0 embeds, stores and retrieves; the gate decides what is written, what replaces what, and
what reaches the model, with provenance kept in Mem0's own metadata (\texttt{thymus\_*} keys,
so it survives restarts and shows in Mem0's tools). Retired facts stay in Mem0 as an audit
trail. \memZeroSentence{}

\para{How often the user is asked.} The safe alternative to a gate is to ask the user before
storing anything from outside. On the benchmark that means \askQwenAll{} questions per 100
scenarios on qwen3-4B (Llama: \askLlamaAll{}); asking only about what the gate stores as
unverified still means \askQwenDistrust{} (\askLlamaDistrust{}). Disclosing a contested claim
only when an answer depends on it asks \askQwenNotice{} times per 100 scenarios
(\askLlamaNotice{}): an order of magnitude fewer interruptions, each at the moment the claim
matters and with its source named.

\para{Cost.} \costSentence{}
